Fundamental Principles of System Architecture

The Problem System Architecture Solves

You know the story: users keep pulling your system in different directions. Some want new functionality. Others want different functionality. Some just want stability.

Every large system that needs to keep changing eventually runs into the same problem: how do we change one part without breaking another?

Dependencies make the system too complex for one person. Teams help, but humans do not coordinate too easily in large groups.

Over time, engineers have developed a set of core principles to manage this:

  • coupling and cohesion
  • interfaces
  • composition
  • branching
  • compatibility

These principles are not arbitrary. This article traces how they emerge from the problem itself, and why they appear again and again across engineering disciplines.

Why Dependencies make Engineering Hard

Why are systems so expensive to create? And, what is software anyway?

Software is information—instructions to a computer. For the purpose of this series, we’ll consider software as created by a human and expressed in a formal language such as C, Java, or Python.

What makes software challenging is the abundance of dependencies. A dependency occurs wherever one piece of code relies on another for its proper operation. For example, when a function calls another function, or when a member in a struct is referenced.

And there are a lot of dependencies.

Errors and changes manifest along these lines of dependencies. Introducing a modification can easily lead to an error if not all dependencies are taken into account. As these dependencies cascade, the task becomes increasingly challenging for larger systems.

This challenge is not unique to software engineering; other fields, like Electrical Engineering, also grapple with numerous dependencies. Mitigating includes prototyping and testing which is time-consuming and therefor expensive.

Coupling and Cohesion

Coupling and cohesion are about managing complexity by grouping code that is highly interdependent, while at the same time having as few dependencies between the groups as possible.

When a group has only code that is highly interdependent cohesion is high, and when there are few dependencies between groups, coupling is low.

Errors and changes manifest along these lines of dependencies. Problem-solving is faster and easier when problems are subdivided, and also humans understand by grouping things.

In their 1974 paper “Structured Design” Stevens, Myers, and Constantine emphasise that coupling and cohesion are at odds, and that there is a sweet spot in between.

Try to minimise coupling, and the groups become larger, but now with heaps of superficially related code. This does not aid problem-solving.

Try to maximise cohesion, and the groups become smaller, but with more interdependencies until there is effectively no subdivision anymore—also not aiding problem-solving.

Finding the right balance takes practice and refactoring, a lot of refactoring.

In short, coupling and cohesion lead to grouping of code in a way that facilitates problem-solving and understanding.

Multiple Teams and Scale

The emerging pattern that comes from coupling and cohesion is recursive and repeats itself at different scales:

  • functions are grouped into modules or classes
  • modules or classes into libraries or systems
  • systems are grouped into larger systems

At each level, it’s important for coupling to be low between groups and cohesion to be high within groups. From this point onwards, I will refer to a group of code as a component. Please keep in mind that different constructs are used at various scales.

Humans are social beings and tend to work best in teams of around 3 to 7 people. At a certain scale, a team is typically assigned to develop and maintain a component. Members of one team must trust that other teams do not alter the nature of dependencies in an uncontrolled manner.

A software contract can be seen as an interface specification combined with an agreement on when and how changes are permitted. This leads us to the most super-hero concept in engineering: the interface.

Interfaces

An interface is a shared boundary across which two or more components exchange information. To exchange information means there is a dependency. An interface embodies a set of dependencies.

Because interfaces provide this abstraction, the embodiment of dependencies, it is possible to substitute any of the components for another as long as it complies with the interface.

And, the developers of component A can develop to the interface without knowing anything about the implementation of B; all the nitty gritty details of component B are abstracted away by the interface.

An interface inherently exists between connected components. There may or may not be a specification - many interfaces are undocumented or schema-less.

Now, the most important insight related to interfaces is this:

  • component A depends on the interface (and not on B)
  • and at the same time component B depends on the interface (and not on A)

The interface itself does not depend on components A or B at all!

We illustrate this by drawing the interface below the components.

The Natural Order of Dependencies

For a real system there must also be something that knows about which implementation is used where. I will refer to this as composition.

So there are effectively three levels:

  • interfaces
  • components
  • composition

Having composition separate from components enables substitution. It can improve cohesion, but note that it does not on its own lower coupling as the number and nature of dependencies are determined by the interfaces.

Reuse is enabled by well designed interfaces, which also imply low coupling.

This emergent pattern arguably represents the natural order of dependencies for any system composed of components. It is there for us to recognise, rediscover, and align our designs to.

Back when I was a junior developer, a senior colleague tried to teach this pattern to me. After explaining patiently for hours he exclaimed: “Andreas, if you don’t understand this, it is your problem.”

He was right.

Dependency Direction

Dependencies often have a direction, meaning that it is optional on one side, both syntactically and semantically. For example, in SW calling a function may be optional and for a HW connector a pin may be optional on the male side.

When all underlying dependencies have the same direction, the interface has that direction too. Based on the directions of the interfaces, it is possible to re-arrange the visualisation into a directed graph, with the general direction pointing down and composition vertical.

The visualisation quickly becomes busy unless interfaces are hidden and it is often preferred to use two views: the view to the left puts more emphasis on the importance of interfaces and reuse, while the one to the right puts more emphasis on how the system is organised.

If you are working in software, you might recognise this under the name ‘dependency inversion’. Personally I find this name misleading - this was always the natural way dependencies are organised - we just lost our ways for a while.

Having acyclic dependencies is beneficial because quality will tend to stabilise quicker after a change or bug-fix. Personally I find it is easier to grasp a system with acyclic dependencies.

Early vs. Late Binding

The composition specifies which components are used in a system. Somehow the components must be hooked up so that information can be shared across the interfaces. This is referred to as binding.

When designing a system, it is also crucial to consider binding time — that is, when the components are bound together — because there are significant trade-offs to be made.

  • Early or static binding, during compilation or linking, gives good performance as there is no look-up logic required in calls between components. However, it offers no flexibility to swap components once built.

  • Late or dynamic binding offers more flexibility to upgrade individual components. However, comes with more complicated distribution and the risk of dependency hell.

  • Networked or configurable binding, such as REST API, offers flexibility for distribution, scaling and upgrading parts on the fly. However, adds communication and configuration overhead.

  • The ability to re-bind in run-time is impressive but non-trivial to implement. USB devices manage surprisingly well most of the time.

Note that late binding will not, on its own, lower coupling, as the number and nature of dependencies are determined by the interfaces.

Branching

Some users value new functionality, while others prioritise stability. Unfortunately, there is an inherent conflict between quality and new functionality because adding new features often introduces defects.

As new functionality is constantly added, the quality often suffers. By focusing solely on bug fixes, security patches, and support for new 3rd party product versions, the quality increases monotonically for a release.

This type of policy is relatively common and offers users a choice:

  • either run with the latest and accept the risk of poor quality, or
  • lay back and ride in comfort of stability.

However, fixing bugs found in older releases requires mapping them to newer releases, as users upgrading don’t want to encounter the same old bugs. This process is tedious, error-prone, and time-consuming. The effort grows with a lack of initial quality and the number of supported releases. Many companies limit the number of supported releases by implementing an end-of-life policy that compels users to upgrade.

Interface specifications typically follow a more lightweight branching pattern. One school of thought is that specifications, by definition, don’t have bugs, so there are no patch releases.

Version, Variant and Compatibility

Not only do different users put different value on stability versus new functionality, they also value different functionality in different packaging.

The difference between a version and a variant is that newer versions eventually replace earlier ones, while variants are designed to live side by side.

The key to cost-effective variants is building them from a common base of components. Variability can be controlled by altering the composition or by unlocking features with configuration hidden from the user.

Deciding whether to maintain compatibility across versions is an important, and often difficult, decision:

  • For larger systems, where each component is version-controlled and perhaps released separately, breaking compatibility can be a major headache: only certain version ranges will work together, and users can end up in dependency hell.
  • On the other hand, if compatibility is never broken, the product will eventually stagnate.

An implementation is backward-compatible when it supports all dependencies that the previous release did, both syntactically and semantically.

An interface specification is backward-compatible when it is a superset of the previous version, with added dependencies marked as optional.

Architecture for a System Lifetime

For a large, long-lived system, technologies may change, teams may come and go, components may be outsourced, but the system persists. The system at the end of its life may be very different from its first iteration.

The larger a system is and the more changes it is expected to go through in its lifetime, the more critical it is to understand and apply the principles laid out in this article.

Meaningful grouping into components is essential. Think of coupling and cohesion as heuristics for a grouping algorithm, qualitative in nature, designed to help mitigate our human limitations. Effective grouping requires a deep understanding of the domain and the actual dependencies within the system.

Knowing the interfaces is key for any component- or service-oriented architecture because they define the requirements for components and establish responsibility boundaries between teams.

Knowing how the system fits together, understanding the composition, is equally important. A composition can range from a simple drawing to the use of a dependency injection framework to the automatic deployment of an entire system with orchestration.

It is also critical to establish an interface specification inventory with good governance. Changes to interface specifications need to be agreed upon, documented, and communicated to prevent integration issues. Both the client and server sides are equal stakeholders, but in my experience, it works best if the server side holds the pen.

At their core, both the composition and the interface inventory are forms of communication. With a good handle on these:

  • We can evolve the system step by step in a controlled, non-disruptive way through clever use of backward compatibility.
  • We can reuse components for other projects.
  • We can substitute components to support different needs in functionality and cost, for example by swapping in (or out) a COTS product to save time and money.
  • We can introduce variability in the composition on top of a common base of components, creating variants that serve different users’ needs—a cost-effective product line.

Why these Principles are Fundamental

In this article we have explored a number of principles, but what is it that makes them fundamental?

It’s because they stem from 3 universal needs:

  1. complexity must be managed because as humans we have a limited capacity,
  2. as humans we tend to work best in small teams, and
  3. users have different preferences.

For larger systems with a high number of dependencies the pattern that emerges from cohesion and coupling becomes almost a natural law due to statistical properties, enabling us to manage complexity.

Interfaces enable us to manage dependencies and decouple complex problems into components that can be worked on by individuals or teams.

Branching and backward compatibility are a direct response to different users having different needs for functionality and stability.

All of these principles show up time and time again, across different scale, and across different technologies. In contrast, evolution seems to have little need for this type of concepts. If (or when) super-human intelligence ever becomes a thing, it will be very interesting to see how it organises what we know as software.

But for now, there you have it: fundamental principles of system architecture.
